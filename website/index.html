About me

I did not take the straight road into computer science. In 2017 I started an honours degree in pure mathematics at the University of Calgary and tutored math and French on the side. I left in 2019, moved to Banff, and spent close to four years serving tables at Earls. After that I led a property care crew for a summer.

In 2023 I came back to school at Mount Royal University, this time for computer science. The math never left. I have kept taking it alongside my computing courses: abstract algebra, cryptography, numerical analysis, linear algebra. What changed is that I found the part of computing where math and machines meet, which is the low-level end: how a processor decides what to run next, how C turns into assembly, how a program talks to hardware.

In the summer of 2026 I worked as a research assistant at Mount Royal, extending a C++ simulator used to study processor scheduling. That summer confirmed what I want to do.

Experience

Summer 2026
Mount Royal University

Research assistant, processor scheduling

A speed scaling processor can change how fast it runs. Going faster finishes jobs sooner but costs more energy, so there are two decisions to make: which job runs next, and how fast. I worked on a C++ simulator built to study those decisions.

My job was to extend it. I took size-based scheduling policies from published papers and implemented them in the simulator, working inside a code base that was about ten years old. Then I ran hundreds of simulations to test whether the results in the literature held up against the schedulers the simulator already had. I presented the work at Mount Royal's student research conference, and I plan to continue it as my senior project.

Fall 2026, in progress
Computing Machinery II

In the Pocket, a rhythm game for the Atari ST

A team project written in C89 for a computer from 1985. Notes travel along a single music staff, each lane on its own line of the staff, and the player has to hit them in time. We decided early that songs would not contain chords, which keeps both the playing and the code simpler.

My part is the game model: the code that holds the state of the game, kept separate from drawing and input. On a machine this small, nothing is hidden from you. There is no library to lean on for graphics or timing, so every design decision shows up directly in the code.
